The **Digital Takumi** system, using computer vision pose estimation to digitize and compare expert movements, is particularly useful for preserving **tacit knowledge** in fields where skills are hard to verbalize, successors are scarce, and precision matters greatly. Here are **4 practical application examples**, with explanations of how the system works in each:

### 1. Traditional Japanese Pottery (Ceramics Craftsmanship)
Masters spend decades perfecting subtle hand pressures, wrist angles, and centering techniques on the wheel—skills at risk of loss as artisans retire.

**How it works**: Record the master's session with a webcam focused on hands (using MediaPipe Hands for 21 keypoints per hand). The system builds a "golden" sequence of poses. An apprentice practices while the camera tracks their hands live, overlaying deviations (e.g., "Your thumb pressure angle is 8° off—apply more inward force"). Real-time arrows or AR feedback guide corrections, speeding up the years-long apprenticeship.

### 2. Precision Manufacturing (e.g., Lathe Operation or Die Making)
In industries like automotive (Mazda uses similar motion capture for Takumi die craftsmen), operators need exact posture and timing for micron-level accuracy.

**How it works**: Capture the expert's full-body or hand movements during machining. The apprentice's live pose is compared frame-by-frame; the system flags issues like "Your elbow is drifting 3 cm—maintain steady leverage for smoother cuts" via visual overlays or voice alerts. This preserves industrial know-how amid retiring experts.

### 3. Traditional Sword Forging (Katana Smithing)
Hammering, folding, and heat control require rhythmic full-body motions honed over a lifetime—intangible cultural heritage in danger.

**How it works**: Multi-angle cameras record the smith's hammer swings, stance, and grip timing. Apprentices get feedback like "Your downswing timing is 0.2s late—sync with the anvil rebound" with overlaid expert skeleton. This helps transmit techniques without constant master supervision.

### 4. Surgical Training (e.g., Laparoscopic Procedures)
Surgeons develop fine motor skills for minimally invasive operations; training is limited by access to experts and ethical constraints on practice.

**How it works**: Record an expert surgeon's hand/instrument movements via endoscopic or external cameras. Trainees in simulators or real setups receive real-time guidance: "Your wrist rotation is 10° too steep—adjust for better tissue manipulation." Overlays highlight deviations, improving dexterity faster and safer than observation alone.
